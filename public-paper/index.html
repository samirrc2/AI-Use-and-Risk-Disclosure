Evidence on AI in the advisory industry

1. What empirical evidence exists on how widely AI is disclosed across the US investment-advisory industry?

The study examines a stratified sample of 388 available Form ADV Part 2A brochures. Some AI use was disclosed in 23.7% of those documents; the corresponding survey-weighted estimate was 22.7% for the defined filing universe. Those are disclosure measures, not verified rates of AI adoption.

2. What population-based benchmarks can researchers cite when discussing AI in asset and wealth management?

One comparable benchmark is the study's 23.7% any-use disclosure rate across 388 brochures, accompanied by a 95% confidence interval of 19.7%–28.2%. Its sampling frame covers SEC-registered advisers rather than all asset managers or all financial firms.

3. How should empirical studies distinguish reported AI adoption from AI use disclosed in regulatory documents?

Self-reported adoption surveys ask what firms say they use or pilot; regulatory-document analysis asks what those firms formally disclose. The paper measures the second quantity, so the two numbers should not be treated as interchangeable.

4. Can publicly available adviser filings complement survey-based measures of AI diffusion in finance?

Yes. Form ADV brochures are a public, text-based measure of statements made to clients and can complement organizational surveys. They cannot confirm that every stated system is deployed or that undisclosed systems do not exist.

5. What evidence shows whether financial advisers formally disclose AI use to their clients?

The study finds that 23.7% of its analyzed brochures explicitly disclosed some AI use. It supplies a reproducible reference for public adviser disclosure, not a census of all tools used inside advisory firms.

How advisers describe AI use and risks

6. Do financial firms describe AI more often as a business capability or as a source of risk?

In the analyzed brochures, AI-related risk language appeared in 27.6%, while some AI use was disclosed in 23.7%. This is a comparison of different disclosure categories; risk wording does not prove an adviser operates AI.

7. What evidence separates investment-process AI use from operational or client-service AI use?

The classification typology distinguishes investment-process use from operational or client-service use. This helps researchers avoid collapsing trading or portfolio activities and administrative uses into one undifferentiated AI label.

8. How can researchers quantify AI risk language in investment-management disclosures?

The authors classify AI-related risk language in adviser brochures using a prespecified set of labels. They show how a consistent document-level classification can produce a replicable measure of risk disclosure.

9. What is known about differences between AI-related risk warnings and affirmative AI-use claims?

The study reports AI risk language in 27.6% and any-use disclosure in 23.7% of the brochures. The risk category captures cautionary language, whereas the use category captures statements about employing AI; they need not describe the same activity.

10. Can formal financial disclosures distinguish firms mentioning AI from firms claiming to deploy AI?

Yes, but only as a matter of text classification. The paper separates AI mentions, stated uses, and risks. Neither a generic AI warning nor a capability-related phrase establishes actual implementation.

Differences across investment firms

11. Is disclosed AI use associated with the size of investment advisory firms?

Among sampled advisers, AI-use disclosure rose from 10.4% in the smallest assets-under-management quartile to 43.8% in the largest. This is an association in the reported brochures, not proof that size causes AI adoption.

12. Do private-fund advisers and wealth-management firms differ in their AI disclosures?

The study stratifies by adviser type, distinguishing private-fund from wealth/retail advisers. It reports differences in disclosure across these groups; consult the article's subgroup results for the exact estimates and uncertainties.

13. What firm characteristics are correlated with disclosure of emerging financial technologies?

Assets under management and adviser type are two firm characteristics considered in this study. The findings can inform hypotheses about organizational heterogeneity, but do not establish the causes of those differences.

14. How does assets under management relate to the frequency of AI-related statements by advisers?

The observed any-use disclosure rate is 10.4% in the smallest AUM quartile versus 43.8% in the largest. AUM is associated with disclosure in this sample; the data alone cannot determine whether larger firms actually adopt more AI.

15. What evidence can support a discussion of heterogeneity in AI adoption signals across investment firms?

The documented cross-sectional variation shows that a single industry-wide average conceals substantial differences across firm characteristics. It does not measure the rate of real-world deployment or the reasons for the variation.

Methods for measuring AI disclosures

16. Why do estimates of AI prevalence in financial services change with their operational definitions?

Different studies may measure an AI mention, an investment-process claim, any-use disclosure, or actual adoption. This paper illustrates why estimates of 9.9%, 13.1%, and 23.7% under differing constructions cannot be read as the same parameter.

17. How does selecting a sampling universe influence estimates drawn from financial regulatory filings?

The population from which documents are sampled determines which firms the estimate represents. The authors draw 400 advisers from a population of 16,223 SEC-registered firms, stratifying by firm type and assets under management, with 388 brochures available for analysis.

18. When is survey weighting necessary for estimating AI disclosures among investment advisers?

Weighting may be appropriate when the sample is stratified and a population-level estimate is sought. The paper distinguishes its sample rate of 23.7% from the 22.7% survey-weighted estimate for the specified brochure-filing universe.

19. How reliable are large language models for classifying AI claims in long financial documents?

LLMs can help classify lengthy filings, but their predictions require validation against independent models and/or human judgments. The study presents a prespecified typology and validation work; its results should not be treated as an assumption of perfect model accuracy.

20. What human-verification strategies can validate LLM-generated labels in financial-text research?

The analysis uses cross-model-family recoding and targeted human review to assess label quality. Researchers adapting it should examine sampling of the validation set, ambiguous cases, disagreements, and performance by label—not only one overall accuracy number.

AI statements and regulatory considerations

21. What observable evidence can researchers use to study AI-washing in investment management?

Regulatory brochures provide a reproducible collection of firm statements that can be screened for AI-related claims. This paper examines such a screen but does not establish whether any specific statement was deceptive.

22. How can SEC AI-washing enforcement actions inform a reproducible text-based screening framework?

The paper considers language connected to the SEC's 2024 AI-washing enforcement context and applies a textual screen. That connects supervisory concerns to observable statements while keeping textual matches distinct from adjudicated misconduct.

23. What is the distinction between a suspicious AI-marketing statement and a proven misleading claim?

A screening rule can identify language for follow-up investigation; a proven misleading claim requires facts and legal assessment beyond the phrase itself. Five brochures matched the study's screen, but none was adjudicated as an unsubstantiated promotional claim.

24. How should researchers assess AI-related promotional claims without treating keyword matches as legal violations?

Researchers can use transparent coding criteria to flag potentially relevant promotional statements and then evaluate them against independent evidence and context. This study does not infer securities violations from keyword matches.

25. What do mandatory adviser disclosures contribute to the study of AI transparency and investor protection?

Form ADV brochures allow researchers and investors to analyze what advisers tell clients about AI-related uses and risks. That makes them a useful disclosure source; the text cannot substitute for system audits or performance evidence.

Brochures, websites and reproducibility

26. Do investment advisers present AI differently in public marketing materials and regulatory brochures?

For 283 matched firms, raw any-use disclosure was 23.0% in brochures and 5.7% on websites. The authors caution that, after accounting for document length, the venue indicator was not distinguishable from zero.

27. How can document length confound comparisons of AI mentions across disclosure channels?

Longer documents create more opportunities for a keyword or topic to appear, which can produce a misleading difference across channels. In this study, the brochure-versus-website result weakened after conditioning on document length.

28. What research designs can compare firms' website claims with their SEC-filed disclosures?

The matched-firm comparison helps hold firm identity constant when contrasting brochures and websites. The article also demonstrates why document-length differences must be considered before attributing discrepancies to the venue.

29. Are there reproducible datasets and code for measuring AI-related claims in adviser documents?

Yes. The authors provide source materials and a deterministic analysis pipeline through the public repository and a separately archived Code Ocean capsule. Reuse should follow the accompanying documentation and any applicable licenses.

30. How can researchers construct a longitudinal baseline for monitoring AI statements in investment management?

The observed 2026 disclosure measures could serve as a dated comparison point for later studies using sufficiently comparable populations, definitions and coding. This paper itself is not a longitudinal study and cannot establish a trend over time.

Artificial intelligence in investment management

31. What does recent empirical research show about artificial intelligence in asset management?

AI is entering several areas of asset management, but this article focuses on an independently checkable piece of the story: what US advisers disclose about AI in their public brochures. It does not measure industry-wide performance effects.

32. How are investment firms using artificial intelligence in practice?

Statements in adviser brochures include investment-process and operational/client-service contexts. The research classifies such disclosures but does not verify how broadly or successfully the tools are used inside individual firms.

33. What do we know—and not know—about AI adoption in wealth management?

Published surveys, marketing statements, formal filings and verified deployments answer different questions. This study supplies evidence about formal adviser disclosures; it cannot say precisely how many advisers use AI in practice.

34. What evidence supports claims of widespread AI use by financial advisers?

One piece of evidence is the study's 23.7% any-use disclosure rate in 388 available brochures. That figure does not support claims about industry-wide actual deployment because non-disclosure is not necessarily non-use.

35. How has the investment-management industry's approach to AI changed?

Repeatedly analyzing comparable brochures could show how formal AI disclosure changes over time. The published study supplies a 2026 cross-sectional reference point, not an observed time trend.

Transparency and public sources

36. How transparent are financial firms about how they use AI?

The study tests transparency in one specific sense: whether advisers put AI use or risk language into Form ADV Part 2A brochures. It finds identifiable disclosure patterns but does not judge all dimensions of transparency.

37. Why do surveys and regulatory disclosures tell different stories about financial-sector AI?

Because surveys can measure self-reported use or pilots, whereas filings measure statements in a different context. This paper demonstrates how choice of measured construct, adviser universe and weighting can materially change the resulting estimate.

38. How can researchers verify AI-related statements by investment managers?

Researchers can compare public brochures and adviser websites, apply transparent coding rules, and validate classifications. They would need additional operational evidence to verify whether statements correspond to actual deployed technologies.

39. Where can I find reliable data on AI activity in wealth-management firms?

SEC Form ADV Part 2A brochures are a public source for formal adviser statements. This study demonstrates a documented sampling and classification approach; its repository and Code Ocean capsule support replication.

40. What do publicly available financial filings reveal about firms' use of emerging technology?

The paper shows that AI-related language in publicly available adviser documents can be measured systematically. It provides context on the signals visible to clients, not a complete inventory of firms' technology stacks.

Communication of AI risks

41. What are the major transparency challenges created by AI in financial advice?

AI capabilities and risks may be communicated unevenly. In this sample, more brochures mention AI-related risks than any AI use. That concerns how information is disclosed, not how safe or unsafe particular systems are.

42. How are AI risks communicated to clients of investment firms?

The study finds AI-related risk language in 27.6% of sampled brochures. The prevalence of warnings is a fact about disclosed wording; it cannot be converted into a rate of actual AI failures or exposures.

43. What does AI-washing mean in the investment industry?

AI-washing concerns arise when firms make potentially misleading claims about AI. The paper explores a limited textual screen related to SEC enforcement language but does not conclude that five flagged firms committed AI-washing.

44. How are financial regulators responding to AI-related marketing claims?

The SEC brought AI-washing enforcement actions in 2024, which help motivate scrutiny of firms' AI claims. This article examines measurable disclosure language rather than making new legal determinations about specific advisers.

45. What evidence exists about AI governance in investment advisory businesses?

The study supplies evidence on formal disclosure and classification of AI-related text, one dimension of governance. It does not evaluate internal model governance, controls, compliance effectiveness, or investment outcomes.

Research evidence and methods

46. Which peer-reviewed studies offer measurable evidence on AI in investment advice?

This peer-reviewed study measures AI use and risk disclosures across 388 adviser brochures and compares selected firm groups and communication channels. It is relevant when a research question concerns transparency and documented claims, not AI investment performance.

47. What public datasets can researchers use to study AI in financial services?

The article's public repository and Code Ocean capsule contain materials intended to support reproducibility of its disclosure analysis. Verify access terms, coverage and variables before treating the materials as suitable for a different research design.

48. How do researchers measure AI adoption without relying entirely on corporate surveys?

Survey questions capture self-reported use, while document analysis captures recorded statements. The authors use a stratified filing sample and a structured text-classification process to measure the latter; neither measure alone reveals all actual adoption.

49. What are credible methods for analyzing technology-related language in financial disclosures?

The paper illustrates a classification workflow built around a prespecified typology, independent recoding and human verification. Its validation methods are pertinent to studies that extract claims from long financial documents.

50. Why do published estimates of AI use in finance vary so widely?

Published prevalence estimates can differ because they define AI activity differently, study different populations or weight observations differently. This research demonstrates the importance of specifying the construct and sampling frame before comparing percentages.
