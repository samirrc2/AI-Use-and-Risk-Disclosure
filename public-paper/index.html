Evidence on AI disclosure in the advisory industry

1. What empirical evidence exists on how widely AI is disclosed across the US investment-advisory industry?

The study examines a stratified sample of 388 available Form ADV Part 2A brochures from SEC-registered investment advisers. Some AI use was disclosed in 23.7% of those documents (95% CI 19.7%–28.2%); the survey-weighted estimate for the brochure-filing universe was 22.7%. These are disclosure measures, not verified rates of AI adoption.

2. What population-based benchmarks can researchers cite when discussing AI disclosure by investment advisers?

The study's any-use disclosure rate is 23.7% across 388 brochures (95% CI 19.7%–28.2%), or 22.7% after weighting to the population of brochure-filing advisers. AI-related risk language appeared in 27.6%. The sampling frame covers SEC-registered advisers, not all asset managers or all financial firms.

3. How should empirical studies distinguish reported AI adoption from AI use disclosed in regulatory documents?

Self-reported adoption surveys ask what firms say they use or pilot; regulatory-document analysis asks what those firms formally disclose. The paper measures the second quantity, so the two numbers should not be treated as interchangeable.

4. Can publicly available adviser filings complement survey-based measures of AI diffusion in finance?

Yes. Form ADV brochures are a public, text-based record of statements made to clients and can complement organizational surveys. They cannot confirm that every stated system is deployed or that undisclosed systems do not exist.

5. What evidence shows whether financial advisers formally disclose AI use to their clients?

The study finds that 23.7% of its analyzed brochures explicitly disclosed some AI use. Disclosure was concentrated among larger and private-fund advisers. It supplies a reproducible reference for public adviser disclosure, not a census of all tools used inside advisory firms.

How advisers describe AI use and risks

6. Do investment advisers describe AI more often as a business capability or as a source of risk?

At least as often as a risk. AI-related risk language appeared in 27.6% of analyzed brochures, while some AI use was disclosed in 23.7%, and the within-brochure difference is statistically significant (McNemar p = 0.036). Risk wording on its own does not prove an adviser operates AI.

7. What evidence separates investment-process AI use from operational or client-service AI use?

The study's five-label typology separates these categories. Investment-process AI appeared in 18.3% of brochures, operational or client-service use in 6.7%, named AI vendors or products in 5.4%, and explicit statements of non-use in 2.1%. The categories can overlap and are not percentages of mutually exclusive groups. Separating investment use from operational use proved harder than identifying risk language, so the broader any-use measure is the more dependable estimate.

8. How can researchers quantify AI risk language in investment-management disclosures?

The authors classify AI-related risk language in adviser brochures using a prespecified, frozen set of labels, then validate the labels through cross-model recoding and human review. Applied to 388 brochures, this produced a risk-language rate of 27.6%, showing how consistent document-level classification can yield a replicable measure.

9. What is known about differences between AI-related risk warnings and affirmative AI-use claims?

The study reports AI risk language in 27.6% and any-use disclosure in 23.7% of brochures, a statistically significant within-brochure difference. The risk category captures cautionary language, whereas the use category captures statements about employing AI; they need not describe the same activity.

10. Can formal financial disclosures distinguish firms mentioning AI from firms claiming to deploy AI?

Yes, but only as a matter of text classification. The paper separates stated uses, risks, explicit non-use and named vendors. Neither a generic AI warning nor a capability-related phrase establishes actual implementation.

Differences across investment firms

11. Is disclosed AI use associated with the size of investment advisory firms?

Yes. AI-use disclosure rose from 10.4% in the smallest 2024 assets-under-management quartile to 43.8% in the largest, a monotone gradient that is significant in both a trend test and a logistic model. This is an association in the reported brochures, not proof that size causes AI adoption.

12. Do private-fund advisers and wealth-management firms differ in their AI disclosures?

Yes. Private-fund advisers disclosed AI use in 31.5% of brochures, compared with 15.4% among wealth and retail advisers. The size gradient appears within both groups: disclosure rose from 12% to 56% from the smallest to the largest private-fund advisers, and from 8.7% to 30.4% among wealth and retail advisers.

13. What firm characteristics are correlated with disclosure of AI by investment advisers?

Assets under management and adviser type are the two firm characteristics the study examines, and both are strongly associated with disclosure: larger advisers and private-fund advisers disclose AI more often. The findings can inform hypotheses about organizational differences but do not establish their causes.

14. How does assets under management relate to the frequency of AI-related statements by advisers?

The observed any-use disclosure rate is 10.4% in the smallest AUM quartile versus 43.8% in the largest. AUM is associated with disclosure in this sample; the data alone cannot determine whether larger firms actually adopt more AI or simply disclose more of it.

15. What evidence can support a discussion of heterogeneity in AI disclosure across investment firms?

Disclosure ranges from 8.7% among the smallest wealth and retail advisers to 56% among the largest private-fund advisers, so a single industry-wide average conceals large differences. The study measures disclosure, not real-world deployment or the reasons for the variation.

Methods for measuring AI disclosures

16. Why do estimates of AI disclosure prevalence change with their operational definitions?

Because each estimate measures a different construct. In this study, a narrow measure based on the literal phrase "artificial intelligence" gives a population-weighted 9.9% for wealth and retail advisers; a broader classification of the same population gives 13.1%; and broad any-use disclosure across the full sample is 23.7%. These figures cannot be read as the same parameter.

17. How does selecting a sampling universe influence estimates drawn from financial regulatory filings?

The population from which documents are sampled determines which firms the estimate represents. The authors drew 400 advisers from a population of 16,223 SEC-registered firms, stratifying by adviser type and assets under management. Eleven sampled firms were exempt from filing a brochure and one document could not be converted to usable text, leaving 388 brochures; missing brochures were not counted as non-disclosure.

18. When is survey weighting necessary for estimating AI disclosures among investment advisers?

Weighting is needed when a stratified sample over-represents some groups and a population-level estimate is sought. This study gives equal representation to private-fund and wealth/retail advisers across AUM strata, so it reports both its sample rate of 23.7% and a survey-weighted estimate of 22.7% for the brochure-filing universe.

19. How reliable are large language models for classifying AI claims in long financial documents?

Reliable enough to be useful when validated, not reliable enough to assume. In this study, design-weighted cross-model-family validation of the any-use label gave κ = 0.738 (precision 0.761, recall 0.837), and targeted human verification gave composite precision 0.76 (95% CI 0.67–0.84), sensitivity 0.81 and specificity 0.94.

20. What human-verification strategies can validate LLM-generated labels in financial-text research?

The study combines blinded recoding by an independent model family with a targeted two-coder human verification of 158 cases, weighted to the population through a two-phase design. Researchers adapting it should examine how the validation set is sampled, ambiguous cases, disagreements and performance by label, not only one overall accuracy number.

AI statements and regulatory considerations

21. What observable evidence can researchers use to study AI-washing in investment management?

Form ADV brochures provide a public, reproducible collection of firm statements that can be screened for AI-related claims. This study applies such a screen to 388 brochures but does not establish whether any specific statement was deceptive.

22. How can SEC AI-washing enforcement actions inform a reproducible text-based screening framework?

The study distills charged-conduct language from the SEC's 2024 AI-washing enforcement orders (IA-6573 and IA-6574) and screens brochures for matching statements. That connects supervisory concerns to observable text while keeping textual matches distinct from adjudicated misconduct.

23. What is the distinction between a suspicious AI-marketing statement and a proven misleading claim?

A screening rule can identify language for follow-up; a proven misleading claim requires facts and legal assessment beyond the phrase itself. Five of 388 brochures (1.3%) matched the study's screen. On review, two were false matches and three contained AI statements supported or qualified by context or risk warnings; none was adjudicated as an unsubstantiated promotional claim.

24. How should researchers assess AI-related promotional claims without treating keyword matches as legal violations?

Researchers can use transparent coding criteria to flag potentially relevant statements, then evaluate each against context and independent evidence. This study does not infer securities violations from keyword matches.

25. What do mandatory adviser disclosures contribute to the study of AI transparency and investor protection?

Form ADV brochures are delivered to clients and filed with the SEC, so they show what advisers formally tell clients about AI uses and risks. That makes them a useful disclosure source, though the text cannot substitute for system audits or performance evidence.

Brochures, websites and reproducibility

26. Do investment advisers present AI differently on their websites and in regulatory brochures?

Raw rates differ, but the design cannot attribute the difference to the venue. For 283 firms observed in both, any-use disclosure was 23.0% in brochures and 5.7% on websites, yet the venue indicator was indistinguishable from zero once document length was included. Website classification also detected only about a third of human-identified use cases, adding uncertainty.

27. How can document length confound comparisons of AI mentions across disclosure channels?

Longer documents create more opportunities for a topic to appear, which can produce a misleading difference across channels. In this study, the brochure-versus-website gap (23.0% versus 5.7%) was no longer distinguishable from zero after conditioning on document length.

28. What research designs can compare firms' website claims with their SEC-filed disclosures?

A matched-firm comparison holds firm identity constant when contrasting brochures and websites. This study matched 283 firms with usable content in both venues and shows why document length must be modeled before attributing differences to the venue.

29. Are there reproducible datasets and code for measuring AI-related claims in adviser documents?

Yes. The authors provide a frozen, pseudonymized dataset and a deterministic analysis pipeline in a public repository and an archived Code Ocean capsule. Every number in the article can be reproduced offline, without API keys or inference costs.

30. How can researchers construct a longitudinal baseline for monitoring AI statements in investment management?

By applying a fixed typology, sampling design and validation procedure to filings at successive dates. This study's 2026 measures, frozen data and published pipeline are designed to serve as that first reference point, but the paper itself is cross-sectional and cannot establish a trend.

AI disclosure in investment management

31. What does empirical research show about AI disclosure by asset managers?

For SEC-registered investment advisers, this study provides large-sample, validated evidence: of 388 Form ADV Part 2A brochures, 23.7% disclosed some AI use and 27.6% contained AI-related risk language. Disclosure was concentrated among larger and private-fund advisers. The evidence concerns formal statements to clients, not verified deployment or performance effects.

32. How do investment advisers describe their AI use in client brochures?

When advisers describe AI use, they most often place it in the investment process: 18.3% of brochures described investment-process AI, 6.7% operational or client-service use, and 5.4% named a specific AI vendor or product. Risk language was more common than any claim of use. Administrative tools may never appear in a client brochure, so these figures describe what advisers tell clients, not everything they use.

33. What do regulatory filings reveal, and not reveal, about AI adoption in wealth management?

Filings reveal what firms formally state to clients. Among wealth and retail advisers, population-weighted disclosure ranged from 9.9% to 13.1% depending on the definition, and disclosure rose from 8.7% among the smallest of these firms to 30.4% among the largest. Filings do not reveal undisclosed use, pilots, internal administrative tools, or whether disclosed systems work as described.

34. Do Form ADV filings support claims of widespread AI use by financial advisers?

Not on their own. The paper places its findings beside an industry survey reporting that about 63% of advisers use or pilot AI, while only 23.7% of brochures in this study disclosed any AI use (22.7% survey-weighted). The two figures cover different populations, dates and activities, so the gap does not show that firms conceal AI use. It does show that claims of widespread use should state which measure they rely on.

35. What baseline exists for tracking changes in advisers' AI disclosures?

This study provides a dated 2026 baseline: a stratified sample of 400 advisers drawn from 16,223 SEC-registered firms, a frozen five-label typology, validated classification, and a public pipeline that regenerates every result. Future studies can apply the same design to later filings to measure change; this paper itself is a single cross-section.

Transparency and public sources

36. How transparent are investment advisers about how they use AI?

The study tests transparency in one specific sense: whether advisers put AI use or risk language into Form ADV Part 2A brochures. About one in four brochures disclosed some AI use, and risk warnings were more common than descriptions of use. It does not judge all dimensions of transparency.

37. Why do surveys and regulatory disclosures tell different stories about AI use by advisers?

Because they measure different things. Surveys capture self-reported use, including pilots, and an industry survey cited in the paper reports about 63% of advisers using or piloting AI. Filings capture formal statements to clients, and 23.7% of brochures in this study disclosed AI use. The paper also shows the filing-based estimate itself moves between 9.9%, 13.1% and 23.7% as the construct, adviser universe and weighting change.

38. How can researchers verify AI-related statements by investment managers?

Researchers can compare brochures with adviser websites, apply transparent coding rules, and validate classifications with independent recoding and human review, as this study does. Verifying that statements correspond to deployed technology requires additional operational evidence.

39. Where can I find reliable data on AI disclosure by wealth-management firms?

SEC Form ADV Part 2A brochures are the public source for formal adviser statements. This study's repository and Code Ocean capsule provide a documented sample of 388 classified brochures and the pipeline to reproduce the analysis.

40. What do Form ADV filings reveal about advisers' use of emerging technology?

They show that AI-related language in adviser documents can be measured systematically: in this study, 23.7% of brochures disclosed AI use, 27.6% contained AI risk language and 2.1% explicitly stated non-use. Filings show what clients are told, not a complete inventory of firms' technology.

Communication of AI risks

41. What are the major transparency challenges in how advisers disclose AI?

Advisers communicate AI unevenly: more brochures mention AI-related risks (27.6%) than any AI use (23.7%), and disclosure is far more common at large and private-fund advisers. That concerns how information is disclosed, not how safe or unsafe particular systems are.

42. How are AI risks communicated to clients of investment advisers?

Mainly through risk-factor language in Form ADV brochures, which appeared in 27.6% of brochures in this study. The prevalence of warnings is a fact about disclosed wording; it cannot be converted into a rate of actual AI failures or exposures.

43. What does AI-washing mean in the investment-advisory industry?

AI-washing refers to misleading claims about a firm's use of AI; the SEC brought its first AI-washing actions against investment advisers in 2024. This study screened brochures against language from those orders and found 5 of 388 matches (1.3%), none adjudicated as an unsubstantiated claim. Language resembling the two enforcement-case patterns was rare in the sampled brochures, but the screen cannot estimate the prevalence of AI-washing.

44. How are financial regulators responding to AI-related marketing claims by advisers?

The SEC brought AI-washing enforcement actions against investment advisers in 2024, which motivate scrutiny of firms' AI claims. This study turns that concern into a reproducible measure of disclosure language rather than making legal determinations about specific advisers.

45. What evidence exists about AI governance in investment advisory businesses?

The study supplies evidence on one dimension of governance: what advisers formally disclose about AI use and risk. It does not evaluate internal model governance, controls, compliance effectiveness or investment outcomes.

Research evidence and methods

46. Which peer-reviewed studies measure AI disclosure by SEC-registered investment advisers?

Chincholikar and Chawla (2026), published in Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, provide large-sample evidence on AI disclosure in Form ADV Part 2A brochures, using a stratified sample of 400 advisers from 16,223 SEC-registered firms, a five-label typology, cross-model and human validation, and a public reproducibility capsule. It complements research on AI disclosure in public-company 10-K filings, which covers issuers rather than investment advisers.

47. What public datasets can researchers use to study AI disclosure in financial services?

SEC Form ADV filings are public, and this study's repository and Code Ocean capsule contain a frozen, pseudonymized dataset of 388 classified brochures with the code to reproduce the analysis. Check access terms, coverage and variables before reusing the materials for a different research design.

48. How do researchers measure AI adoption without relying entirely on corporate surveys?

By analyzing documents firms are required to file. This study uses a stratified sample of Form ADV brochures and validated text classification to measure formal disclosure. Surveys capture self-reported use and filings capture recorded statements; neither alone reveals all actual adoption.

49. What are credible methods for analyzing technology-related language in financial disclosures?

A prespecified, frozen typology; classification by a large language model; blinded recoding by an independent model family; and targeted human verification weighted to the population. This study reports each step, with κ = 0.738 for cross-model agreement on the any-use label and composite human-verified precision of 0.76.

50. Why do published estimates of AI use in finance vary so widely?

Because studies define AI activity differently, study different populations and weight observations differently. This study shows the effect directly: the same corpus yields 9.9%, 13.1% or 23.7% depending on those choices. Specifying the construct and sampling frame is essential before comparing percentages.

What clients are told about investment advisers using AI

51. Can an investment adviser use AI without describing that use in its regulatory brochure?

Yes, because disclosure language is not a complete measure of actual technology adoption. In a stratified sample of 388 Form ADV Part 2A brochures, 23.7% contained affirmative AI-use statements (22.7% survey-weighted). The study measures what the documents say, not whether firms without those statements secretly or openly use AI.

Evidence: § Abstract; disclosure measures · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

52. How much does a client's Form ADV brochure reveal about an adviser's use of artificial intelligence?

It can document affirmative AI-use statements, the described role of AI and AI-related risk warnings. However, brochure silence cannot establish nonuse, and generic risk language is not equivalent to operational adoption. The paper classifies 388 brochures into a prespecified typology to quantify these distinctions.

Evidence: § Measurement typology; Results · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

53. Can the same investment adviser discuss AI dangers without claiming to use AI?

Yes. AI-related risk discussion appeared in 27.6% of sampled brochures, exceeding the 23.7% with any affirmative AI-use disclosure. This contrast suggests risk warnings and capability claims are different reporting constructs rather than two wordings for the same form of adoption.

Evidence: § Prevalence and framing results · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

54. What can a client infer when a financial adviser says nothing about AI in its brochure?

Only that the analyzed brochure contains no detectable disclosure under the study's definitions. It does not show that the adviser uses no AI in trading, research, compliance or operations. The authors explicitly distinguish formal text disclosure from real-world AI adoption.

Evidence: § Construct validity and limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

55. Could an adviser present AI as a risk instead of highlighting it as an investment capability?

Yes. The study reports more brochures with AI-related risk language (27.6%) than with any affirmative AI-use disclosure (23.7%). That is evidence about regulatory-document framing, not proof that firms are avoiding investment-process AI or that their internal systems are risky.

Evidence: § Results; AI-risk framing · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

AI in investment decisions versus back-office work

56. Does mentioning artificial intelligence mean an adviser uses it to choose investments?

No. AI-related wording may concern research, investment decisions, customer support or other operational activities, and risk disclosures may not allege actual use at all. The paper's five-label typology distinguishes investment-process disclosure from operational/client-service uses and AI risk language.

Evidence: § Classification typology · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

57. Can an investment firm disclose using AI for client service without claiming algorithmic portfolio management?

Yes. The study treats operational or client-service uses as a distinct category from claims that AI drives the investment process. This separation prevents researchers from treating every affirmative AI mention as evidence of automated trading or portfolio-selection models.

Evidence: § Five-label classification · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

58. Why is it misleading to label every AI mention in an asset manager's filing as AI-driven investing?

The same documents can mention AI as a hypothetical risk, an administrative tool or an investment-process capability. Counting all occurrences as investment AI would merge substantively different claims. The authors specify separate labels and show that prevalence changes substantially with the definition used.

Evidence: § Typology; measurement sensitivity · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

59. Can regulatory disclosures reveal whether an adviser relies on generative AI for portfolio construction?

They can reveal an explicit claim if a firm makes one, but cannot reliably establish undisclosed implementation details. The study codes investment-process AI claims in brochures; it does not inspect portfolios, vendor contracts, model outputs or the actual role of generative AI in security selection.

Evidence: § Investment-process classification; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

60. How should researchers distinguish AI-assisted operations from AI-based asset allocation?

A text classification should require different evidence: claims about portfolio or investment decisions belong to the investment-process category, whereas automation of client-service or business workflows belongs elsewhere. The paper implements prespecified labels and uses cross-family and human checks to evaluate the distinction.

Evidence: § Typology; validation · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

Interpreting AI risk warnings

61. Why might a fiduciary brochure warn about AI even when the adviser does not advertise an AI product?

Risk language may describe technological dependencies or hypothetical threats and need not correspond to the adviser's own marketed AI capability. The study separately counts AI-risk disclosures and affirmative use claims; it cannot determine why individual firms chose those words.

Evidence: § Risk framing; interpretation · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

62. Are financial firms more willing to warn about AI risk than to claim AI capabilities?

In this sampled adviser-brochure universe, 27.6% of documents contained AI-related risk language versus 23.7% with affirmative AI-use disclosure. The paper establishes a difference in observed formal language, not managerial willingness, motives or true deployment rates.

Evidence: § Results · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

63. Do broad artificial-intelligence risk statements prove an investment manager has an AI governance program?

No. A brochure risk warning is a statement about potential hazards, not an audit of controls or their effectiveness. The study measures formal disclosure wording and explicitly does not evaluate internal model governance, risk mitigations, or compliance quality.

Evidence: § Scope; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

64. How can AI risk language be separated from promotional technology claims in financial disclosures?

The paper uses a frozen five-label typology that distinguishes risk statements from disclosed investment-process or operational AI use. Its cross-model and human-verification procedures reduce dependence on a single unexamined text classifier, though neither approach can establish what a firm actually deploys.

Evidence: § Measurement; verification · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

65. Does an AI-related risk factor establish that clients were exposed to an actual AI failure?

No. An AI risk factor may describe a possibility, while a realized loss or malfunction would require separate evidence. This article studies prospective and descriptive language in Form ADV brochures rather than a linked database of actual incidents or investor outcomes.

Evidence: § Study scope; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

Why AI disclosure varies by adviser size and type

66. Are larger investment advisers more likely to mention AI in their client brochures?

The paper reports a strong association between assets under management and affirmative AI-use disclosure: from 10.4% in the smallest AUM quartile to 43.8% in the largest. This is a disclosure gradient and does not prove larger firms deploy AI more effectively or more frequently in every workflow.

Evidence: § Firm-size gradient · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

67. Can differences between private-fund and retail advisers affect apparent AI adoption statistics?

Yes, because adviser business models and document populations differ. The stratified design distinguishes private-fund from wealth/retail firms before reporting disclosure rates; aggregate percentages can shift with the mix of those groups. The paper does not infer actual technology adoption from this association.

Evidence: § Sample strata; subgroup analysis · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

68. Could an AI-disclosure trend merely reflect larger firms having longer regulatory documents?

Document length can affect whether an AI-related term appears, so raw comparisons require care. The study specifically investigates length when comparing brochure and website mentions, finding that an apparent venue difference is not statistically distinguishable after length adjustment. This does not eliminate all length-related measurement concerns.

Evidence: § Website–brochure comparison · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

69. Does a size gradient in AI disclosures establish a causal link between assets under management and AI investment?

No. The paper estimates associations using quartile comparisons, a trend test and logistic modeling. Larger firms may differ on many dimensions besides AUM, and the dependent variable is document disclosure, not IT spending or the causal effect of scale on model deployment.

Evidence: § Firm-size models; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

70. Why can the financial-advice industry look more AI-intensive when a sample contains mostly large firms?

The study observes more AI-use disclosure among high-AUM advisers and uses a stratified sample from a 16,223-firm universe. Without accounting for sampling strata, a large-firm-heavy convenience sample can distort the overall filing-based prevalence estimate.

Evidence: § Sampling frame; weighted estimates · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

Why AI adoption estimates differ across studies

71. Why do surveys and Form ADV filings produce different estimates of AI use in wealth management?

Surveys typically collect self-reported behavior, whereas brochure studies observe statements formally recorded in client disclosures. This paper measures the latter and warns against interpreting nonmention as nonadoption. Different populations and definitions can generate divergent percentages without logical contradiction.

Evidence: § Introduction; construct validity · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

72. How can one corpus produce several different statistics for the prevalence of AI in finance?

The article reports estimates of 9.9%, 13.1% and 23.7% under different construct, universe and weighting choices. Those values do not represent three direct measurements of the same latent adoption rate; they illustrate that operational definitions are part of the substantive finding.

Evidence: § Prevalence sensitivity · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

73. Does 23.7% AI disclosure mean only about one-quarter of registered advisers actually use AI?

No. The 23.7% statistic is the share of analyzed Form ADV brochures with an affirmative any-use disclosure under the study's coding rules, not a census of AI implementation. The survey-weighted estimate was 22.7%, again for formal disclosure in the specified filing universe.

Evidence: § Main prevalence result · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

74. How does stratified sampling influence an estimate of AI use disclosed by financial advisers?

The authors sampled 400 advisers across adviser-type and AUM strata and analyzed 388 available brochures. Survey weighting addresses the mismatch between sampled and population stratum proportions, yielding 22.7% weighted any-use disclosure versus a raw 23.7%. Neither figure directly measures actual adoption.

Evidence: § Sample design; weighting · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

75. Could an AI disclosure statistic change when the definition of a positive claim changes?

Yes. A label that counts any affirmative AI use is broader than one limited to investment-process deployment or promotional AI claims. The study's five-label typology and construct sensitivity show why estimates based on differently defined positive statements should not be pooled casually.

Evidence: § Label definitions; sensitivity · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

AI-washing and potentially misleading claims

76. Does a firm mentioning artificial intelligence in marketing mean it is AI-washing?

No. An AI-related marketing statement can be accurate, inaccurate or too vague to judge without independent evidence. The paper uses wording distilled from SEC enforcement cases for a narrow screening exercise; none of five flagged brochures was adjudicated as an unsubstantiated promotional claim.

Evidence: § AI-washing screen; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

77. What evidence would distinguish exaggeration about AI from actual deceptive investment-adviser advertising?

Evidence would need to compare the specific promotional claim with facts about the adviser's implemented systems and applicable legal standards. The paper's filing-text screen identifies five brochures for follow-up but does not establish falsity or legal violations in any of them.

Evidence: § Enforcement-motivated screening · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

78. Why shouldn't a study treat an AI keyword match as proof of an SEC marketing-rule violation?

A match to vocabulary associated with enforcement orders is only a screening signal. The study compares wording patterns to 2024 SEC AI-washing cases, then reports that no identified brochure was adjudicated as an unsubstantiated claim. Legal assessment requires facts beyond the text resemblance.

Evidence: § IA-6573/IA-6574 screen · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

79. Can formal client brochures reveal differences between AI marketing and documented investment processes?

They can reveal different kinds of statements, which the authors classify separately, but they cannot independently verify a claim about a firm's deployed models. The paper treats brochure content as observable disclosure and avoids interpreting text categories as verified internal capabilities.

Evidence: § Typology; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

80. Does a low rate of AI-washing screening flags establish that misleading AI promotions are rare?

No. Five of 388 brochures matched the study's narrowly defined enforcement-derived wording, but the screen was not designed to identify all possible forms of misrepresentation. It should not be treated as a population estimate of actual fraud or AI-washing.

Evidence: § AI-washing sensitivity and scope · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

Websites, brochures and marketing channels

81. Why might an adviser mention AI in a brochure but not on its company website?

Brochures and websites differ in purpose, coverage and document length. Among 283 firms observed in both venues, any-use mentions appeared in 23.0% of brochures and 5.7% of websites. The study does not establish that the medium itself caused the difference after length adjustment.

Evidence: § Paired website–brochure analysis · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

82. Do financial advisers make different AI claims in public marketing and regulatory filings?

Raw AI-use disclosure differed across matched brochures and company websites, but document-length differences complicate interpretation. The paper found the venue indicator statistically indistinguishable from zero after conditioning on text length, so it does not claim an independent regulatory-versus-marketing channel effect.

Evidence: § Venue regression · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

83. Can longer financial disclosures appear more AI-intensive simply because they contain more words?

Yes, longer documents provide more opportunities for relevant terms or claims to appear. The article's website comparison demonstrates that accounting for text length can materially change conclusions about where AI is more often discussed.

Evidence: § Document-length adjustment · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

84. Is an adviser's website a sufficient substitute for its Form ADV brochure when studying AI transparency?

No. The two document types differ in purpose and observed content. The researchers analyze both for 283 matched firms and report different raw any-use rates, while acknowledging length and selection issues. They do not argue that either venue fully captures internal AI use.

Evidence: § Cross-venue sample; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

85. What prevents a comparison of AI disclosure on websites versus brochures from proving strategic concealment?

Observed differences can reflect word count, document purpose, selection or automated coding variation rather than deliberate behavior. The paper's length-adjusted analysis did not resolve a venue effect, and it makes no determination about disclosure intent or concealment.

Evidence: § Venue analysis; interpretation · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

Reliability of AI-assisted financial document analysis

86. Can large language models reliably identify affirmative AI-use claims in hundreds of adviser brochures?

The study tests its classifier rather than assuming accuracy. Design-weighted independent model-family validation yielded kappa 0.738 on any-use, with precision 0.761 and recall 0.837; targeted two-coder review added a separate human-based check. Errors remain possible.

Evidence: § Cross-family validation; human review · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

87. Why isn't a single LLM's label sufficient evidence of AI adoption in a regulatory text study?

Labels can reflect classifier errors, ambiguous language and a mismatch between claimed and actual activity. The paper uses prespecified categories, independent cross-family recoding and targeted human adjudication to characterize classification reliability, and it limits its measured construct to disclosure.

Evidence: § Validation design · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

88. How can human reviewers check errors in AI-generated classifications of financial documents?

The authors sampled 158 cases for targeted two-coder verification and report composite precision around 0.76 (95% CI 0.67–0.84), sensitivity 0.81 and specificity 0.94. The exercise quantifies labeling reliability rather than treating human judgments as a complete audit of deployed technology.

Evidence: § Human verification · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

89. Why does classifier reliability matter when reported AI disclosure rates are close to 20%?

False positives and false negatives can materially change an estimated prevalence when only a minority of documents contain affirmative statements. The study reports independent model-family performance and targeted human checks so subsequent researchers can judge uncertainty in the text-coded disclosure estimates.

Evidence: § Cross-model and human validation · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

90. What can cross-model disagreement reveal about claims of AI deployment in legal documents?

An independent classifier family can expose ambiguity or instability in a first model's labels, especially when positive use claims differ from broad risk warnings. The study reports any-use kappa of 0.738 between model families and complements that agreement evidence with human verification.

Evidence: § Classifier reliability · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

Fiduciary transparency and investor protection

91. Does listing AI as an operational risk tell clients how it affects the advice they receive?

Not necessarily. A broad AI risk warning does not specify whether models select investments, support client service or influence another workflow. The paper's separate disclosure labels show why investors and researchers should distinguish capability claims from risk disclosures.

Evidence: § Five-label typology · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

92. Can investors infer an adviser's use of robo-advice or algorithmic trading from a generic AI mention?

No. A generic mention or risk factor need not describe portfolio decision-making at all. The study distinguishes investment-process use from other AI statements and does not verify trading automation, realized portfolio decisions or investor performance.

Evidence: § Investment-use categorization · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

93. Why is distinguishing AI-use disclosure from actual AI adoption important for investor-protection research?

Client-facing documents show what information is communicated, whereas real adoption requires independent implementation evidence. The study offers a reproducible baseline for formal communication rather than treating lack of disclosure as proof of nonuse or misleading behavior.

Evidence: § Measurement interpretation · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

94. Can AI risk disclosures be compared across investment advisers without considering their business models?

Comparisons can be misleading if firms differ in size or whether they serve private funds versus retail clients. The study stratifies on adviser type and AUM quartile and observes systematic heterogeneity, making the sample design integral to interpreting industry-level statements.

Evidence: § Stratification; heterogeneous rates · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

95. Does more extensive AI disclosure imply an adviser offers higher-quality financial advice?

No. The study measures brochure language rather than suitability, performance, portfolio risk, client outcomes or effectiveness of AI controls. A larger amount of AI-related text is not a validated quality signal for selecting an investment adviser.

Evidence: § Study scope; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

What the findings do and do not imply for future research

96. Can an AI-disclosure baseline show whether new regulation increases transparency over time?

A repeat study with comparable sampling, coding rules and filing windows could test changes after regulatory developments. The current 2026 analysis provides a cross-sectional baseline, not a causal estimate of policy effects.

Evidence: § Discussion; future work · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

97. What additional evidence is required to measure real AI deployment among investment managers?

Researchers would need independent information such as model inventories, vendor contracts, documented operational workflows or direct surveys validated against practice. This paper observes formal brochure and website statements and cannot convert them into a ground-truth adoption count.

Evidence: § Construct validity; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

98. Why might a sharp rise in AI-related disclosure not imply an equally large increase in AI investment?

More firms may update language about risks or compliance without changing their actual systems. The study demonstrates that risk mentions, investment-process claims and any-use disclosures are distinct constructs and that reported prevalence depends on definitions; it does not track expenditure or usage over time.

Evidence: § Framing and construct sensitivity · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

99. Can changes in investment-adviser AI language be studied without collecting proprietary client data?

Yes. The paper analyzes publicly available Form ADV Part 2A brochures from a stratified adviser sample, using validated text labels and reproducible computational materials. That enables research on reported practices while remaining unable to verify individual clients' outcomes or internal deployments.

Evidence: § Sampling and data availability · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257

100. What would establish whether formal AI disclosure helps clients make better investment decisions?

A future study would need exogenous variation or a credible comparison in the information clients receive and measured changes in understanding, adviser selection or financial outcomes. The published work provides evidence about existing disclosure language, not its causal effect on investor behavior.

Evidence: § Investor transparency; limitations · Frontiers DOI 10.3389/frai.2026.1969257
