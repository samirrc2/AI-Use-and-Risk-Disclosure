AI in wealth management: what are firms telling investors?

Financial advisers increasingly talk about artificial intelligence. But how much of that use do firms actually disclose to clients? A peer-reviewed study of 388 SEC filings separates AI adoption claims from formal disclosures—and finds that AI risk warnings appear more often than stated AI use.

Samir Chincholikar & Robin Chawla · Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence · 14 October 2026

How is AI changing wealth management and financial advice?

AI may support investment research, portfolio analysis, risk management, client service and everyday operations. But reporting that an adviser uses AI is different from showing how extensively it uses the technology, how well it works, or what it tells clients. This study examines a concrete slice of that larger debate: the AI-related claims and warnings in public disclosure brochures filed by US investment advisers.

Is AI adoption in wealth management overhyped?

It depends on what is being measured. Industry surveys can count firms that report using or piloting AI; brochure analysis counts the AI activities firms formally describe. The published paper cites a 2026 Charles Schwab survey reporting about 63% of surveyed adviser firms using or piloting AI, while this study finds 23.7% of analyzed brochures disclose AI use. These figures come from different samples and definitions; the gap is not an estimate of exaggeration, hidden adoption, or misconduct. Read the peer-reviewed analysis and definitions.

How transparent are financial advisers about their AI use?

Public adviser brochures provide one checkable source of evidence. Among the 388 brochures analyzed, AI-related risk wording was more common than explicit disclosure of AI use. The research also compares brochures and public websites for 283 matched firms, while warning against treating their raw differences as effects of the communication channel. Transparency in a specific document is not a direct measurement of actual adoption or system performance.

Can investors trust claims about AI-powered investment services?

AI-related marketing should be evaluated against evidence about how a technology is used, rather than relying on an AI label alone. This study offers a reproducible way to classify adviser disclosures and a limited screen based on language associated with SEC AI-washing enforcement actions. None of the five screening matches was adjudicated as an unsubstantiated promotional claim, so the paper cannot establish which investment firms are misleading clients or how common AI-washing is.

What questions does this research not answer?

It does not determine whether AI produces better investment returns, whether AI advisers outperform humans, whether any particular firm actually uses AI, or whether any adviser has violated a securities law. For those questions, different types of evidence are needed. What this paper contributes is evidence about disclosure, risk language, and measurement uncertainty in US investment-adviser filings.

What does the evidence from SEC filings actually show?

How many US investment advisers disclose using AI?

The researchers drew a stratified random sample of 400 SEC-registered advisers from a population of 16,223 firms, spanning private-fund and wealth/retail adviser types and 2024 assets-under-management quartiles. Of the 388 available brochures, 23.7% disclosed AI use (95% confidence interval, 19.7–28.2%). The survey-weighted estimate for the defined brochure-filing universe was 22.7%.

For context, self-reported adoption surveys, actual deployment inside firms, and legally relevant public disclosures measure different things. This research studies the last of these, using advisers' Form ADV Part 2A filings.

Are AI risks disclosed more often than AI capabilities?

In the sampled brochures, 27.6% contained AI-related risk language while 23.7% disclosed some AI use. The within-brochure difference was statistically significant (McNemar p = 0.036). A risk warning alone should not be interpreted as proof that the adviser actually deploys AI.

Are larger investment advisers more likely to disclose AI?

The observed any-use disclosure rate increased from 10.4% in the smallest AUM quartile to 43.8% in the largest, and was higher among private-fund advisers. This finding concerns an association in the analyzed disclosures; it does not demonstrate a causal effect of firm size.

Why do AI disclosure estimates vary?

One central result is methodological: the study reports values of 9.9%, 13.1%, and 23.7% when the measurement construct, adviser universe, and weighting approach change. Those percentages should not be treated as interchangeable estimates of the same target. The paper validates its LLM-based measurement with blinded cross-family re-coding and human review.

Can SEC Form ADV brochures reveal AI-washing?

The study tested a textual screen derived from language relevant to the SEC's 2024 AI-washing enforcement actions. Five brochures matched the screen, but none was adjudicated as an unsubstantiated promotional claim. These findings do not establish a population-wide rate of AI-washing or wrongdoing by any named investment adviser.

What about the AI claims on advisers' websites?

For 283 matched firms, the researchers found raw any-use disclosure rates of 23.0% in brochures versus 5.7% on websites. However, after conditioning on document length, the venue indicator was not distinguishable from zero. The raw gap should not be attributed to the venue itself.

How can researchers reproduce the findings?

The authors provide a frozen, pseudonymized dataset and deterministic analysis scripts through the GitHub repository and a Code Ocean computational capsule. The archived capsule is distinct from the DOI of the peer-reviewed journal article.
